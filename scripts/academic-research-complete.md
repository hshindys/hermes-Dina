## 2️⃣ World-Building (بناء العوالم)

### Definition
World-Building is the process of creating a fictional world with consistent rules, settings, social systems, and sensory detail. It applies to ALL fiction — not just fantasy/sci-fi.

### The 5-Step Method (Harvard Creative Writing Studio)

| Step | Action |
|------|--------|
| 1 | Leave your world — recognize fiction is distinct from reality |
| 2 | Determine what you cannot do in reality — set your world's rules |
| 3 | Create characters within the world — they interact with it uniquely |
| 4 | All Things Go — every idea must be consistent and plausible |
| 5 | Napoleonic conquest — you own this world, dominate it |

### World-Building Checklist
**Physical**: Geography, climate, architecture, nature, seasons, sensory detail (sight/sound/smell/touch/taste)
**Social**: Political/economic systems, class/hierarchy, religion, customs, food/clothing, language/dialects
**Character**: How they interact with physical + social world, cultural norms affecting behavior, language register per character

### Jinn World-Building (Arabic Fantasy Specialization)
- Jinn = invisible parallel civilization with own kingdoms
- Kingdoms by element: air, water, earth, fire
- Rules governed by Quran and Hadith — cannot contradict divine law
- Story engine: Jinn-Human interaction and conflict
- Language: Formal/archaic for Jinn, colloquial for humans

### Key Sources
- University of Chicago Creative Writing Studio: "Research and World-Building in Fiction"
- Writing Cooperative: "A Guide for Successful World-Building in Fiction" (Sarah Frances Hicks)
- David Foster Wallace: *Broom of the System* — world-building model
- Wole Soyinka: *Chronicles from the Land of the Happiest People on Earth* — allegorical world as critique

## 3️⃣ Short-Form Screenwriting (90-Second Series & TV Scripts)

### Golden Rule
**1 page of script = 1 minute of screen time**
- 90-second script = ~1.5 pages
- Write tight. Every word earns its place.

### Standard Format
```
FADE IN:

INT. LOCATION — TIME OF DAY

Action description (4-5 lines max).

CHARACTER NAME
Dialogue line.

(Character direction in parentheses)
```

### Three-Beat Structure (90 Seconds)
1. **Setup** (0-20 sec): World, character, situation
2. **Conflict** (20-60 sec): Problem, tension, character choice
3. **Resolution** (60-90 sec): Punchline, twist, or emotional beat

### What Makes 90-Second Scripts Work
- ONE idea. Not three.
- Strong hook in first 5 seconds (visual, question, conflict, mystery, or action)
- Visual storytelling — show, dont tell
- Sound design matters (describe sounds in action lines)
- End on a memorable, resonant beat

### TV Pilot vs. Short Episode
- **Pilot**: Establishes the series — characters, world, ongoing conflict
- **Short Episode**: Self-contained story with beginning, middle, end

### Visual Storytelling Principles
- Describe what the camera sees
- Use camera directions sparingly (director decides)
- Focus on CHARACTERS and their actions
- Every frame should tell a story even without dialogue

### Common Mistakes
- Too much dialogue for the time limit
- Exposition that kills momentum at the opening
- Writing "We see" or "We hear" — just describe what happens
- Characters TALKING ABOUT feelings instead of SHOWING them

### Arabic Screenwriting Notes
- **Egyptian Arabic** for character dialogue
- **Fusha (formal Arabic)** for narration, poetic/meditative passages
- **Archaic Arabic** for Jinn/supernatural characters
- **Egyptian colloquial** for human characters in dialogue
- **Fusha mixed with colloquial** for inner monologue

## 4️⃣ Literary Criticism (النقد الأدبي)

### What is Literary Criticism?
Literary criticism is the study, evaluation, interpretation, and analysis of literature. It aims to understand meaning, evaluate quality, locate works within traditions, and reveal hidden layers of significance.

### Types of Literary Criticism

| Type | Arabic | Description | Key Concepts |
|------|--------|-------------|--------------|
| Close Reading | النقد الداخلي | Focus on text itself — language, structure, imagery | Mark patterns, repetitions, contradictions |
| Structural | النقد البنيوي | Analyze narrative structure — voice, timeline, POV | Genette narratology, Todorov fantastic mode, Propp morphology |
| Contextual/Historical | النقد السياقي | Place text within its history and culture | Historical period, author biography, genre conventions |
| Comparative | النقد المقارن | Arabic vs. Western traditions | Cross-cultural narrative analysis |
| Psychological | النقد النفسي | Character/author psyche | Jungian archetypes, Freudian readings |
| Marxist/Feminist/Postcolonial | — | Ideological lens | Power structures, gender, colonialism in texts |

### The Four-Read Method
1. **First Read**: Enjoy it — what struck you emotionally?
2. **Second Read**: Mark key passages — images, phrases, turning points
3. **Third Read**: Analyze structure — how is the text built?
4. **Critical Essay**: Write your argument supported by textual evidence

### Arabic Literary Criticism Key References

**Classical**:
- **Al-Jahiz** (776–868): *Al-Bayan wa al-Tabyin* — masterwork of prose criticism
- **Ibn Abd Rabbih** (860–940): *Al-Iqd al-Farid* — literary anthology with commentary
- **As-Suyuti** (1445–1505): *Science of Badi* (علم البديع) — rhetoric

**Modern**:
- **Sayyid Qutb**: *Al-Naqd al-Adabi al-Hadith* (The Modern Literary Criticism, ~676 pages) — foundational reference
- **Mahmoud M. Hadoun**: *Dirasa fi al-Adab al-Arabi al-Hadith* — comprehensive survey
- **Abd al-Hamid Jaber**: Pioneer of Arabic criticism methodology

**Contemporary Online**:
- Literariness.org: "Arabic Literary Theory and Criticism"
- al-balagha.com: Encyclopedia of Arabic Rhetoric (rhetorical devices encyclopedia)
- Britannica: Arabic literature — literary criticism section
- Boston University Arabic Literature databases
- Cornell University Libraries: Arabic Literature databases

### Arabic Rhetoric Core (البلاغة)
The three foundations of Arabic rhetoric — essential for criticism analysis:

1. **Ma'ani (المعاني)** — How meaning is expressed correctly & persuasively
   - Taqrir (affirmation), Nafy (negation), Istifham (question)
   - Istihlal (resolving contradictions), Ijtimal (combining opposites), Tanahu (paradox)

2. **Bayan (البيان)** — Clarity & figurative expression
   - Haqiqah (literal/true statement) and Majaz (figurative — the most powerful)
   - Majaz sub-types: Istiara (metaphor), Tashbih (simile), Kinayah (indirect), Mulazza (persistent metaphor)

3. **Badi (البديع)** — Aesthetic beautification
   - Simile, Metaphor, Metonymy, Irony, Litotes, Hyperbole, Rhetorical question, Parallelism, Antithesis, Alliteration, Repetition, Brevity (Ijaz), Word-play, Rhetorical chain

## 5️⃣ Arabic Language Capabilities for Fiction Writing

### Balaghah (البلاغة) — The Eloquence System
Arabic rhetoric is arguably the most sophisticated literary-linguistic system in any living language. It is the cornerstone of Arabic literary power.

### Three Components

| Component | Concept | Function |
|-----------|---------|----------|
| **Ma'ani** | المعاني | Correct, precise, persuasive expression of thought |
| **Bayan** | البيان | Clear, vivid, figurative expression of meaning |
| **Badi** | البديع | Aesthetic embellishment — making language beautiful |

### Key Rhetorical Devices for Fiction

**Ma'ani (Thought/Meaning) Devices:**
- **Istitlak** (احتجاج): Using a word to draw attention to something hidden
- **Istihlal** (حلّ): Resolving apparent contradictions for deeper meaning
- **Ta'til** (تعطيل): Deconstruction — revealing a terms limitations

**Bayan (Expression) Devices — Majaz family:**
- **Istiara** (استعارة): Metaphor — "The world is a stage"
- **Tashbih** (تشبيه): Simile — "Brave as a lion"
- **Kinayah** (كنية): Indirect reference — "He has a beard" (meaning a bearded man)
- **Maja al-Lafz** (معنى اللفظ): Substituting the literal word for a figurative meaning
- **Iqtida** (اقتضاء): One word necessitating interpretation of another

**Badi (Aesthetic) Devices:**
- **Tamthil** (تمثيل): Synecdoche/metonymy
- **Tajanim** (تجنيس): Coinage of words
- **Taqtiq** (طقطقة): Metaphor with verb of motion for both parts
- **Ijaz** (إيجاز): Brevity — devastating short statements
- **Tasrih** (تصريح): Direct statement with rhetorical punch
- **Mutabaqah** (مطباقاة): Parallelism
- **Tadad** (تضاد): Antithesis / Opposites

### Using Balaghah in Practice
1. **Jinn Characters**: Heavy Badi — archaic vocabulary, rich metaphors, poetic imagery
2. **Human Characters**: Colloquial Egyptian in dialogue → shows authenticity
3. **Narrator (omniscient)**: Formal Fusha with elegant Badi → intellectual authority
4. **First-person narrator**: Personal, less rhetoric, more direct → intimate

## 6️⃣ Egyptian Colloquial in Creative Writing (العامية المصرية)

### The Balancing Act
Too much colloquial alienates formal readers. Too little loses authenticity.
**The Golden Rule**: Egyptian colloquial in dialogue, Fusha in narration.

### The Two Registers

**Fusha (فصحى) — Literary/Standard Arabic**
Use for: Narration, description, internal monologue (literary characters), formal speeches, letters, Jinn/supernatural characters, narrator voice, all poetic passages.

**Egyptian Colloquial (العامية)**
Use for: Direct character dialogue, casual conversations, emotional outbursts, humor, street scenes, working- and middle-class dialogue, informal settings.

### Key Phonological Markers (Signals of Egyptian speech)
| Written | Read | Meaning |
|---------|------|---------|
| إحنا | Aḥna | We |
| إنت | Enta | You (masc) |
| إنتي | Inti | You (fem) |
| هوّا | Huwwa | He |
| هِيّا | Hiyya | She |
| ده | Da | This (masc) |
| دي | Di | This (fem) |
| مش | Mish | Not |
| باش | Bāsh | Will/Going to |
| كده | Keda | Like that |
| أوي | ʿway | Very/A lot |

### Grammatical Features
- **Negation wraps the verb**: ما + verb + ش (e.g., ماكلمش = he didn't speak)
- **Future marker**: باش (e.g., باش أرويج = I will tell you)
- **Progressive**: قاعد + verb (e.g., قاعد أحكي = I'm speaking now)
- **Possession**: بتاع / مال (e.g., الكتاب بتاعي = my book)

### Character Speech Registers
- **Upper class/educated**: Mix Fusha + colloquial with polite markers (سيدي، حيّاكم)
- **Middle/working class**: Predominantly colloquial, Fusha leaks into emotional moments
- **Street/rural**: Heavy colloquial, local idioms and proverbs, informal grammar

### Jinn vs Human Dialogue Template (Chron Novel Pattern)
| Character | Type | Register | Style |
|-----------|------|----------|-------|
| Kron | Jinn (flying) | Formal/Archaic Arabic | Elegant, contemplative, poetic |
| Nourk | Jinn (sea) | Mix Fusha + colloquial | Intelligent, direct, honest |
| Tarek | Human (scientist) | Fusha with scientific tone | Rational, formal, precise |
| Suleiman | Human (working-class) | Egyptian colloquial | Warm, earthy, direct |
| Farid | Human (street) | Egyptian colloquial | Witty, street-smart, fast |

### Literary Models to Study
- **Yusuf Idris**: First Egyptian writer to put dialogue in colloquial — revolutionized short fiction
- **Alaa Al-Aswany**: *The Yacoubian Building* (عمارة ياقوبيان) — masterpiece of mixed registers
- **Naguib Mahfouz**: *The Cairo Trilogy* — Fusha narration with colloquial character voices
- **Salwa Bakr**: Working-class Egyptian women's voices — powerful female narratives
- **Bahaa Taher**: *Sunset Oasis* — political dialogue in colloquial texture
- **Radwa Ashour**: *The Grand Mosque* — Palestinian-Egyptian narrative voice

### Common Mistakes
- ❌ Writing ALL dialogue in colloquial (no contrast = flat reading)
- ❌ Making ALL characters speak the same way (no differentiation)
- ❌ Transliterated Egyptian that is grammatically incoherent
- ❌ Overdoing dialect to the point of cultural exoticism
- ❌ Forgetting that Egyptian colloquial IS a rich literary tradition (not "broken Arabic")

### The Yusuf Idris Lesson
Idris proved that colloquial Egyptian could carry the weight of literary literature. His short stories showed that the "low" register could reveal truths that "high" Fusha sometimes obscured — the lived reality of ordinary Egyptians.
